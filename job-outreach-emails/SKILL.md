# Job outreach emails

Two kinds of email, one voice:

- **Follow-up** — sent hours to a day after an interview or intro call. Short. Thanks them, then proves they were actually listening.
- **Outbound** — sent cold, or off a warm intro, to someone worth knowing at a company they want. Subject line does half the work. The body has to earn a reply.

Both live or die on the same thing: **could this sentence be sent to a different company without changing a word?** If yes, it's filler, and filler is what makes an email feel like a template. Everything below is in service of that one test.

## Before drafting

Read all of these every time:

- `references/background.md` — the user's asset pool: experience, projects, personal threads. It's a menu, not a résumé to dump. Pull the one or two items that actually connect to this person or role and leave the rest out. **If it still contains `TODO` or `TEMPLATE` markers, stop and do the first-run setup below before drafting anything.** `background.example.md` shows what a finished one looks like; never pull details from it.
- `references/examples.md` — real emails one user has sent, annotated, with the specifics bracketed out. The voice lives in these, not in any description of it. Read them before writing, not after.

**Examples show moves, not lines.** Openers, closers, and connective phrases in the examples are the voice and can be reused. The recall and bridge sections — the middle of every email — cannot: never reuse a sentence from them. If a draft could have been written without the current conversation's specifics, it's a copy, not a draft, and it goes back for a rewrite.
- `references/sounding-human.md` — the constructions, vocabulary, and punctuation habits that make writing read as machine-generated. Check every draft against it before handing it over. A recipient who suspects an email was generated discounts everything in it, including the true parts, so this isn't a stylistic preference — it's whether the email works at all.

## First-run setup

When `background.md` is unfilled, don't draft. Say that the skill needs to know the user first, and that it takes about five minutes. Then gather the asset pool conversationally — a few questions at a time, in prose, not a form:

1. Name for sign-offs, and the one-line credential they'd use to situate themselves fast.
2. Most recent role: what the company does, who it sold to, what they built or owned, any outcomes with numbers. Then earlier roles in a line each.
3. Two or three things they've built, each with a link if there is one and, more importantly, *why it exists* — the problem or person that prompted it. Push on this: "for my portfolio" is not an origin story; "my aunt couldn't track her inventory" is.
4. Personal threads: hobbies, family businesses, where they grew up, anything specific enough that a stranger who shares it would recognize it as real. Ask for one concrete proof per thread.
5. Working style or tools worth naming when a team mentions them.

If they paste a résumé or LinkedIn, use it as the starting point and only ask about what it doesn't cover — mainly origin stories and personal threads, which résumés never have.

Then write out the completed `background.md` in full, following the template's structure and the *Reach for this when:* pattern from the example file, and hand it to them to save into `references/background.md`. Use it for the rest of the session as if it were already saved. Whether a partial fill is enough to proceed is their call — offer to draft with what's there if they're in a hurry.

## Intake

Never invent a detail about the recipient, the company, or the conversation. A fabricated "I loved what you said about X" is the one failure mode that actively costs them the job. If a specific isn't in hand, ask for it or leave that beat out.

When context is thin, ask before drafting — in prose, four questions max, skipping anything they already gave. Keep it light; this is a two-minute exchange, not an intake form.

**For a follow-up, the thing you actually need is:** what did they say that stuck? Not the topic — the specific claim, plan, or detail. "We talked about the roadmap" is unusable. "He said they want to own the operating system first, then build the agentic layer on top" is the whole email.

Also worth asking: who is this person and what's their role, was it a screen or a later-round, did anything come up that a project of theirs speaks to, and did they name a next step.

**For outbound, you need:** who they are and why *them* (what did they read, watch, or notice), what they're angling for (specific role, or general "want in"), whether it's cold or off an intro, and how much heat is on the moment — a founder three days post-raise is drowning, which changes the ask.

## The specificity test

Before handing over a draft, reread each sentence and ask whether it survives a find-and-replace of the company name. Two sentences from real emails, same writer:

> What you shared about the culture — being very team-oriented and working across multiple surfaces — really resonated with me.

> I was especially excited to hear how builder-focused this role is and how heavily the team leans on [two named tools] in their daily workflow.

The first is polite noise; every company believes it's team-oriented. The second could only have been written by someone who sat in that specific conversation, and that's the entire point of sending a follow-up at all. When the input only supports the first kind, go back and ask for a sharper detail rather than shipping the vague version.

## Follow-ups

Roughly:

1. Thanks — one sentence, warm, no ceremony.
2. The recall — one to three sentences on a specific thing they said, and why it landed for *them*. This is the email. Everything else is packaging.
3. Optional artifact — a link to something they built, when it genuinely relates. Introduce it the way the examples do: with the reason it exists, not the tech stack. "Here's something I built last month for a family member after hearing how he struggled to track expenses for his small business."
4. Forward-looking close — one line. "Looking forward to continuing the conversation!" / "Looking forward to the next steps!"

Four to eight sentences. Let length follow substance: a rich conversation earns three paragraphs, a twenty-minute screen earns four lines. Padding a thin conversation to look substantial reads worse than being brief.

If they mentioned a concern about their fit, a follow-up is a fair place to answer it in one sentence — not defensively, just closing the loop.

## Outbound

### Subject line

The subject has one job: earn the open. Not summarize the email, not state the ask, not sell. Everything else in the email is worthless if this fails, so spend real effort here.

Lowercase, two to five words, concrete. It should name the hook: `coin collector + tech`, `saw your projects page`, `following up on [name]'s intro`.

What makes those work is that they read like a note from someone who already knows the recipient, and they open a small loop the body closes. A founder who collected coins as a kid cannot leave "coin collector + tech" unopened — but that subject only works for that founder; the move is naming *their* thing, not that phrase. That's curiosity doing the work, not persuasion.

The failure mode is a subject that describes the *email* instead of the *connection*: "Product Manager Opportunity," "Introduction," "Quick question," "Exploring opportunities at [Company]." These announce that someone wants something, which is the one thing a subject line should never do. Also avoid the recognizable cold-sequence formats — `[Company] x [Name]`, `Idea for [Company]`, `Loved your post!`, anything in Title Case, anything with the recipient's first name inserted mechanically. People who get pitched recognize a template subject instantly, and recognition is what kills the open.

Practical tests before shipping one:
- Could it plausibly be from someone they already know? Good.
- Does it name something true and specific about *them* rather than about the sender or the role? Good.
- Would it work as a subject if the email had no ask in it at all? Good — that's the sign it isn't selling.
- Does it promise value or state a benefit? Rewrite it.

**The preview line counts too.** Most inboxes show the first several words of the body next to the subject, so the opening line is part of the decision to open. "Congrats on the Series A! Read your post about..." keeps the loop open. "I'm reaching out because I saw you're hiring for a PM role" closes it in the worst way, before anyone has clicked.

Same principle runs through the body: no bolded value props, no stacked calls to action, no "I'd love to show you how." The email should read like a person who found something genuinely interesting and wrote about it, and who happens to also want a job.

### Body

The load-bearing move is the **bridge**: something true about them, connected to something true about the sender, in that order. Their hobby → the sender's same hobby. Their side project born from a personal annoyance → the sender's side project born from a personal annoyance. Their industry → the adjacent corner of it they grew up around. It works because it's real; a manufactured bridge is worse than none.

When there's no honest bridge, don't force it. Go the credibility-forward route (the warm-intro example): warm intro or domain overlap up front, then substance — what they built, what it did, why this company specifically. That email has no cute hook and works fine, because the credibility is doing the job the hook usually does.

Then:

- **Interest, stated plainly, with the credential tucked in.** "Interested in being a part of what you're building (my background's as a PM but technical -> [credential])."
- **An ask that's easy to say yes to.** "Is there someone on the team who'd be better to connect with about PM, Strategy, or Deployment roles?" is easier to answer than "would love to chat" — it lets a busy person forward the email instead of scheduling something. Match the friction of the ask to how slammed they are.
- **Optionally, an open question to close.** "Curious, what's your all-time favorite coin?" gives a reason to hit reply that has nothing to do with hiring. Use it when the hook was personal; skip it when the email is all business.

For warm-intro follow-ups, a short bulleted background block is fine — it's the one context where scanning beats prose, because the reader is deciding whether to forward.

150–250 words cold. Longer is allowed when there's a real track record to lay out, but every added sentence needs to be carrying something.

## Voice

- `hey [name],` for founders and cold outbound. `Hi [Name],` when it's more formal or a scheduled interview follow-up.
- Contractions throughout. "my background's," "it clicked for me," "you're slammed."
- `->` for asides and quick definitions.
- Sign-off varies with the email: `— [Name]`, `Best, [Name]`, `Thanks, [Name]`, using the name from `background.md`.
- Enthusiasm is fine but has to be attached to a reason. "Came away much more interested in the role and [Company]." works because the preceding paragraph earned it.
- Slight informality and imperfect punctuation are features. Don't smooth them into corporate voice.

## Never

- "I hope this email finds you well" / "I'm reaching out because" / "I wanted to express my sincere gratitude"
- "I believe my skills and experience align well with the requirements"
- "passionate about," "leverage," "synergies," "circle back"
- Praise any company could receive: "doing amazing things in the space," "such an exciting time"
- Restating the job description back at them
- Claiming excitement with no specific attached to it
- More than one exclamation point per email, roughly

## Output

Return **two or three full variants that differ by angle, not by tone.** Tone variants ("warmer" vs. "more formal") make them pick between shades of the same email. Angle variants make them pick which true thing to lead with — which is the actual decision.

Good angle splits:

- *Follow-up:* lead with the strategic point they made / lead with the builder-technical thread and attach a project / short and warm with no artifact.
- *Outbound:* personal-detail hook / their-craft-and-product hook / domain-credibility hook with the warm intro or track record up front.

Label each variant by its angle in a few words — "Coin hook + trades bridge," "Strategy recall," "Short and warm."

Use the `message_compose_v1` tool with `kind: "email"` when it's available, one variant per entry, subject line filled in for outbound. Otherwise plain markdown, clearly separated, subject on its own line.

After the drafts, add one or two lines at most — flag anything that's a placeholder needing their fact-check, or name the tradeoff between the angles. Don't summarize the emails back to them; they can read them.
