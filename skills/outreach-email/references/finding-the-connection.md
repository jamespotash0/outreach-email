# Finding the connection

The best email comes from the best raw material, and the raw material lives in two places: what the recipient has put into the world, and what the user has that genuinely meets it. This step finds both before a word gets drafted. Skip it for follow-ups after a conversation (the conversation is the material), internal emails, client-issue emails, and when the user has already handed over a strong hook.

For **investors**, add their fund's thesis page, portfolio (especially companies adjacent to the user's), partner blog posts and tweets about the user's market, recent deals, and check size and stage. A hook for an investor is usually "you wrote/invested in X, and we're the [specific] version of that bet," not a hobby.

---

## 1. Research the recipient

### With web search or browsing

Search in roughly this order and stop once there are two or three strong hooks:

| Where | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Personal site, "about" page, personal brand site | How they describe themselves, what they choose to highlight, side projects |
| Blog, Substack, Medium, newsletter | A specific argument they made, recently. Their best material is here. |
| Podcasts they've been a guest on (search "[name] podcast") | Origin stories, hobbies, opinions they say out loud but never write down |
| Conference talks, YouTube, webinars | A claim or framework they presented; the Q&A often has the personal bits |
| X / Threads / Bluesky / LinkedIn posts | What they're thinking about this month; anything they asked their audience |
| GitHub, Product Hunt, side projects | What they build for fun, and why |
| Company blog, changelog, press, funding news | What the company just did (trigger events: raise, launch, new market, key hire) |
| Interviews and profiles (local press, alumni magazines, industry sites) | Background: hometown, school, first job, the path to where they are |
| Career history | Past companies, schools, cities, communities; overlaps with the user |

Search variations that surface good material: `"[full name]" [company]`, `"[full name]" podcast`, `"[full name]" interview`, `[name] substack OR blog`, `site:youtube.com "[full name]"`, `"[full name]" talk OR keynote`.

**Verify the person.** Common names collide. Confirm the company and role match before using anything, and prefer sources that link back to their verified profile or company page.

**Prefer recent.** A post from last month beats a talk from 2019. Note the date of everything.

### Without web access

Don't state facts about them from memory. Give the user a short checklist of where to look (the table above, trimmed to the three most likely for this person) and ask them to paste anything that catches their eye: a post, a podcast title, a line from the "about" page. Two minutes of their looking beats a draft built on nothing.

### Stay on the right side of the line

Use what they've put out publicly in a professional or semi-public voice: their writing, talks, podcasts, public posts, public bio, and hobbies *they chose to talk about publicly*. Don't use family members, kids, home location, health, relationships, personal accounts they clearly keep separate, or anything found by digging through old or private material. The test: would they be pleased or unsettled that a stranger mentioned it? A founder who talked coins on a podcast is pleased. Anything that signals surveillance ends the conversation.

## 2. Show the user a hook sheet

Before drafting, show a short list, strongest first. Each hook: what it is, source and date, and which item in `background.md` it could connect to (or "no match yet"). Keep it scannable, five items at most:

```
Hooks for Maya Chen (Fieldnote)
1. Newsletter, Aug 2026: argues trades SaaS wins on payments, not scheduling -> your DealerDesk quoting/accounting work
2. Podcast (Operators Pod, Jun 2026): grew up in her dad's HVAC shop -> your parent's install business
3. Company: launched Fieldnote Pay in July -> no match yet
```

Then mark the one you'd lead with and why, and go straight into the questions below in the same message. If the user said not to ask, pick the top hook, draft, and include the sheet after the drafts so they can redirect.

## 3. Mine the user for the matching half

The research finds their side. Only the user knows the rest. Ask, in prose, two or three of these at most, chosen for the hooks found:

**Something similar**
- "She wrote about [X]. Have you run into that yourself, at work or anywhere else?"
- "Anything you've built, written, or shipped that touches the same problem?"
- "Is there a post, talk, or idea of theirs you actually agree or disagree with? Disagreement, stated respectfully, is often the most interesting email they'll get."

**Common ground**
- "Any overlap: same school, city, previous company, community, hobby, people you both know?"
- "Anyone who could intro you, or whose name you could mention with permission?"

**A way to add value** (the strongest differentiator when it's real)
- "Have you used their product? One specific, honest observation or idea from using it is worth more than any compliment."
- "Is there something small you could make for them: a teardown of their onboarding, a mock of a feature, a quick analysis, a fix to something public?"
- "Do you know someone or something useful to them right now (a candidate, a customer type, a resource)?"

Keep value-adds proportionate: a thoughtful paragraph, a short Loom, or a one-page doc. An unrequested 20-page strategy deck reads as pressure. And it has to be true: never suggest the user claim to have used a product they haven't.

**The angle** (what makes this email unlike the others in their inbox)
- "They described [product] as '[their phrase]'. Any way to turn that around toward what you want?"
- "How long have you been watching them, and what were you doing back then that touched their world?"
- "What's the bold version: the one claim about what you'd do there that you'd happily be held to?"
- "What's their stated goal (a number, a mission, a next market), and where do you fit in getting there?"

If the user has nothing that matches, say so plainly and pick the honest route: a door-opener question about their idea, or credibility-forward. A manufactured connection is worse than none.

## 4. Carry it into the draft

- Lead with the hook that has a real match on the user's side, not the most impressive fact about the recipient.
- One hook per email. A second only if it stacks naturally (the coin email's two bridges). Three reads as a dossier.
- Reference the source lightly ("your post on payments", "on the Operators Pod episode") so it's clear where it came from, which makes it feel like attention rather than surveillance.
- Offer the value-add without strings: "made a quick teardown of the quote flow, happy to send it over" lets them opt in.
- In the note after the drafts, list the sources used with dates so the user can check them before sending.
