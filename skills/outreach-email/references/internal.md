# Internal emails

Emails and messages to people at the user's own company: asks to other teams, notes to execs, status updates, replies, nudges, escalations, pushback, and saying no. Everything in SKILL.md applies except the hook-hunting: the reader already has a reason to read, so the job is to make acting easy.

Register: clear and warm, in the user's voice, but with the ask up front and no cleverness. Match the company's norms when the user shows them (some teams write in lowercase Slack style; some want formal memos).

---

## What to get from the user

- Who's reading, their role, and their relationship to the user (peer, other team, their manager, skip-level exec).
- What exactly the user needs, by when, and **why that date**.
- What the reader gets out of it, or what it saves them.
- What the user will do if there's no answer (the default).
- Email or chat. If they don't say, draft for what they mentioned; offer a chat-length version when the ask is small.

## The structure: bottom line up front

1. **First one or two lines: the ask or the news, with the deadline.** A reader who stops there should know what's needed. "Can your team confirm the /orders rate limit by Wed? Launch copy goes to legal Thursday."
2. **Context, only what they need to act:** a few lines or short bullets.
3. **What's in it for them**, when it's not obvious: "saves your on-call from the support tickets."
4. **A default so silence doesn't block:** "If I don't hear back by Wed EOD, I'll go with 100 req/min from the docs."
5. **Easy reply:** a yes/no, a choice between named options, or "reply 'B' and I'll sign."

Fits on one screen. One email, one ask; a second unrelated ask gets its own email so it can't get lost.

**Subject lines are descriptive** because the reader is triaging: what, and by when. `Need by Wed 9/24: rate limit for /orders launch`. Some companies use prefixes (`ACTION`, `DECISION`, `FYI`); use them if the user's workplace does.

## By situation

### Asking another team (no authority)

People say yes to direct asks far more often than askers expect, so ask plainly rather than wrapping it in apology. Say why it matters to them, keep the ask small and specific, propose a default, and name a reason for the date. Thank them by name afterward, ideally where their manager can see it.

Asking a group: name who you need ("need replies from Priya and Tom by Friday") or everyone assumes someone else will answer.

### Emailing an exec or skip-level

Three to five lines. Lead with the decision or information needed, then the recommendation, then options in one line each, then a link to details. "Need your call on vendor A vs B by Friday to hold pricing. I recommend B: 20% cheaper, same SLA." Never make them dig for the ask.

### Status updates

Lead with status in a word or phrase (on track / at risk / slipping to 10/8), then what changed since last time, then anything needed from the reader ("nothing needed" is useful to say). Bullets are fine here.

### Bad news upward

1. What happened, when, and who's affected, in a couple of sentences.
2. The impact.
3. The fix the user proposes and why it'll work.
4. Who does what by when, and what they need from the reader, if anything.

No burying it under context, no blame. For serious news, the email is the written record of a live conversation: add "happy to talk live today" and suggest the user actually call.

### Replying when you don't have the answer yet

Acknowledge now and commit to a time: "Got it. Need to check with finance; I'll reply by Thursday noon." Then keep the promise.

### Nudging

Resend the request so it stands alone; don't point back to the old email. "Bringing this back up: still need the /orders limit to lock launch copy by Wed. If it's easier, just confirm the 100/min default." Nudge once around the deadline, then escalate.

Never: "per my last email," "not sure if you saw my last email," "as previously stated," "I asked last Tuesday and haven't heard back," "gentle reminder" stacked on a guilt trip. The deadline carries the urgency; a record of their delay only adds friction. These top the list of phrases coworkers find most annoying.

### Escalating

Prefer escalating *together*: tell the other person first, and go to both managers with a joint summary (the disagreement, the options, the tradeoffs, what each side recommends). Unilateral escalation damages the working relationship long after the issue is settled.

CC a manager only when they can decide or need visibility, and say why in the email ("adding Sam since this affects the Q4 plan"). A CC used as pressure reads as pressure.

### Disagreeing or pushing back

State the concern once, specifically, with the evidence, and propose an alternative. If the decision goes the other way, say so plainly: "I'd still do B, but I'm on board with A and will make it work." Keep criticism about the work. For sharp disagreement, suggest a call; email hardens positions.

### Saying no

Give the answer early, tie it to priorities ("I'm focused on shipping A by month-end"), and offer what the user can do: a smaller version, a later date, or a better person to ask.

### Introducing yourself (new hire, new role, new partner team)

Who the user is and their role in one line, why this person specifically (what they work on that the user will depend on), and a small, concrete next step (15 minutes, or "what's the one thing I should read first?").

## Tone

Tone gets misread in email far more often than the writer expects, in both directions. Before handing over anything tense (pushback, escalation, a no), read it in a flat, annoyed voice and fix what sounds curt or sarcastic. Warmth costs a few words and prevents a lot of damage.

- An occasional exclamation point reads as warm without making the writer look less competent. One, not five.
- "Sorry to bother you" and a pile of "just"s waste words; cut them for length, not because apologizing is forbidden.
- Directness norms vary by culture. With colleagues from more indirect cultures, soften criticism ("not quite there yet"); with very direct cultures, hedging can read as unclear.

## Length

Short by default: most internal emails are three to eight lines. Longer when it's a decision memo, a detailed update, or a proposal; then use a short summary up top and headers or bullets below, because internal readers scan.

## Output note

For internal emails, variants split by *approach*, not hook: e.g. "Direct ask with default" / "Softer, gives them the choice", or "Email" / "Slack version". Two is usually enough.
