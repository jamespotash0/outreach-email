# Examples

Real emails, annotated. Read for voice, rhythm, and *moves* — not for lines.

These happen to be job-search and networking emails, but the voice is the user's everywhere: an investor email or an internal ask from this user should sound like the same person, adjusted for the register in SKILL.md's scenario table. The scenario files carry their own skeletons for structure. When the user shares a real email that worked in another scenario, add it here under a new heading, bracketed the same way.

**How to read these.** Openers, closers, and connective tissue are shown verbatim because they *are* the voice. The middle of each email — the recall, the bridge, the substance — is shown with `[bracketed slots]` where the original had a specific. Those slots are the part that must be new every time; the bracket text tells you what kind of detail belongs there. Where a real detail is left in, it's there to show the *level* of specificity, not to be reused.

Never reuse a sentence from a recall or bridge section. If a draft could have been written without the current conversation's specifics, it's a copy, not a draft.

## Follow-ups

### Move: recall one unfakeable detail, attach an artifact

**Version A — software role, tool-usage detail**

> Hey [Name],
>
> I really appreciate you taking the time to chat yesterday. I enjoyed learning more about the role and what [Company] is working on.
>
> I was especially excited to hear how [the specific way they described the role — e.g. how builder-focused it is] and how heavily the team leans on [the specific tool or workflow they named] in their daily workflow. I've spent a lot of time working that way both inside and outside of work, so it made me even more interested in the role.
>
> — Here's something I built [when] for [who] after hearing how [the real-life problem that prompted it]
> [link]
>
> Looking forward to continuing the conversation!
> Thanks,
> [Name]

*Sentence by sentence:* thanks, one line, no ceremony → the recall, where the bracketed detail is the entire email; in the original it was a named pair of AI coding tools, which is unfakeable because it proves they were in the room → "I've spent a lot of time working that way" ties it to the sender in one clause, no résumé → the artifact, introduced by *why it exists*, not what it's made of, arriving as evidence for the claim they just made → flat close.

**Version B — same move, different domain (hardware / ops role)**

> Hey [Name],
>
> Thanks for making the time this morning, really enjoyed it.
>
> The thing that stuck with me was [the specific operational detail they described — e.g. that the field techs still photograph serial numbers by hand and someone re-keys them]. I've spent a lot of time on exactly that kind of gap between what the floor does and what the system thinks happened, so it made the role click for me.
>
> — Here's something I put together [when] after [the real-life prompt]
> [link]
>
> Looking forward to the next steps!
> Thanks,
> [Name]

*What's constant across A and B:* one-sentence thanks; one recalled detail specific enough that swapping the company name would break the sentence; a clause tying it to the sender; an artifact introduced by its reason; a flat close. *What changed:* everything inside the brackets. That's the shape to copy.

### Move: play back their strategy, tie interest to one part of it

**Version A**

> Hi [Name],
>
> Thank you for taking the time to meet yesterday. I enjoyed our conversation and learning more about you and the role!
>
> Strategically, I really like the long-term direction you laid out for [Company]: [the sequencing they described — X first, then Y on top — including any aside they made along the way].
>
> The [specific part of that strategy] is the most exciting part for me because it's where I've spent a lot of my time as [their role], so getting to [that part] is exactly the type of work I want to be doing.
>
> Came away much more interested in the role and [Company].
> Looking forward to the next steps!
> Best,
> [Name]

*Sentence by sentence:* the playback has to be detailed enough that it's obviously not paraphrased from the website — in the original it included the order of operations, a modularization aside, and the founder's own term for the second phase → then interest attaches to a *particular part*, not the whole → "Came away much more interested" is a flat line that only lands because the paragraph above earned it.

**Version B — same move, different domain (marketplace / consumer)**

> Hi [Name],
>
> Thanks for the conversation yesterday, I got a lot out of it.
>
> I keep thinking about the sequencing you described: [their order of operations — e.g. win supply density in one city before touching the demand side, and let the referral loop do the rest]. That's the opposite of how most people in the space seem to be doing it, and it's the version I'd bet on.
>
> The [specific phase] is the part I'd most want to own, because [one sentence on why that maps to what they've done].
>
> Came away much more interested in the role and [Company].
> Best,
> [Name]

### Weak — the one to avoid

> Hi [Name],
>
> It was really nice chatting with you today. I enjoyed learning more about you, the company, and the role.
> What you shared about the culture really: being very team-oriented and working across multiple surfaces, really resonated with me.
> I look forward to the next steps and learning more about [Company]. Please don't hesitate to reach if I can provide any additional information.
> Best,
> [Name]

**Why it falls flat:** swap the company name for any other and the email still makes sense — that's the tell. "Team-oriented" and "resonated with me" are things anyone could write after any conversation. There's no artifact, no reason attached to the interest, and "please don't hesitate to reach out" is boilerplate that adds length without adding anything.

When the available context only supports an email like this, the fix isn't better phrasing — it's going back and asking what they actually *said*.

### The specificity test, side by side

Two sentences, same writer, same email type:

> What you shared about the culture — being very team-oriented and working across multiple surfaces — really resonated with me.

> I was especially excited to hear how builder-focused this role is and how heavily the team leans on [two named tools] in their daily workflow.

The first is polite noise; every company believes it's team-oriented. The second could only have been written by someone who sat in that specific conversation. That's the whole difference.

## Outbound

The coin, projects-page, warm-intro, and product-user emails are the **direct-ask** play: interest stated, ask made. The Duo PM email is the **door-opener** play: no ask, a question about their thinking. Note how length follows the play and the situation: the coin email is short because the hook carries it; the warm-intro email is the longest because the intro bought attention and the track record is the pitch.

### Move: personal-detail hook, stacked bridges, low-friction ask

**Subject:** `[their hobby] + tech`

> hey [name],
>
> Saw the [specific news — the raise, the launch, the post], congrats, that's huge! Somewhere in there I caught that you [the personal detail the sender actually found — a hobby, a first job, a thing they collected], which got me as I [their genuine, matching version of that detail]; [one sentence of proof — where it came from, how long, something concrete enough to be checked].
>
> Likewise, I grew up around [the domain overlap — in the original, a parent's service business]. [One sentence on what it actually does, including the honest caveat about how it differs from what the recipient is building], different corner of the trades but similar problems: [two or three concrete problems that overlap]. So when I read what you're building, it clicked for me.
>
> Interested in being a part of what you're building (my background's as a PM but technical -> [credential]).
> I'm sure you're slammed right now, is there someone on the team who'd be better to connect with about PM, Strategy, or Deployment roles at [Company]? Happy to take it from there.
>
> Either way, congrats again, and nice to meet another [hobby] guy. Curious what's your all-time favorite [hobby object]?
>
> -- [Name]

*Sentence by sentence:* the subject tells the founder something about *themselves*, nearly impossible to ignore → bridge one, with proof (in the original: two specific graded coins, not "I like coins too") → bridge two, with the honest caveat that makes it credible rather than forced → interest plus credential in one parenthetical → the ask assumes they're too busy and offers a redirect, which is easier to grant than a meeting → the closing question is reply-bait that costs nothing.

**Same move, different hobby.** Say the founder mentioned in a podcast that they restore old motorcycles. The subject is `bike restorer + tech`, bridge one is their own restoration project with a specific make and year, the proof is what they're currently mid-rebuild on, and the closing question is "what's the one you'd never sell?" Everything else in the skeleton stays put.

### Move: craft hook — name their project and *why* they built it

**Subject:** `saw your projects page`

> hey [name],
>
> The [specific project on their site] retrospective stuck with me. You built it because [the actual reason they gave — the personal annoyance, the thing they were sick of]: part lesson and part solving your own problem.
>
> Likewise as a builder, I have built many side projects over the last few years. Recently, I built [their project], out of a personal problem, to help me [what it does for them], while getting deeper into [a technical area, six words max].
>
> Interested in being a part of what you're building at [Company] (my background's as a PM but technical -> [credential]). From [their relevant experience], I see how much room [Company] has to run. Would love to connect about PM, Strategy, or Deployment roles at [Company].
>
> Either way, congrats on building a great product.

*Sentence by sentence:* naming one project *and the reason they built it* is a level of attention nobody fakes → the mirror is exact — your side project came from a personal annoyance, so did mine, here it is → the technical parenthetical shows depth in six words instead of a paragraph.

**Same move, different craft.** If the recipient writes a newsletter instead of shipping code, the hook is one specific issue and the reason they said they started writing it; the mirror is something they've written or built for the same reason. The shape doesn't care whether the craft is code.

### Play: door-opener. Move: their idea, your question (no ask)

**Subject:** `Loved your piece on [the specific post by name]`

This one is shown nearly verbatim because it's instructive twice: it got a co-founder to reply in under an hour, and it still has tells worth fixing.

> Hey [Name],
> Came across your recent substack posts, and just wanted to say I really enjoyed your piece on [specific post]. I've been thinking a lot about how [the area the post is about] is evolving, and your framing around [their specific concept] really resonated. It feels like a natural progression — not about eliminating roles, but about maximizing people's value and building more integrated, efficient teams.
>
> Since you framed [their shift] as a "when," not an "if," I'm curious — do you think it works best when it's baked in from the start, like at [their company], or can it be as successful when introduced later into a more traditional org? What do you see as the biggest hurdles or key factors in making that transition work?
>
> When hiring for these roles, do you find folks with a [background A] tend to adapt better, or those coming from [background B] — or does it depend on something else entirely?
>
> Also, do you see this shift as part of a larger cycle that resets over time, or do you think it's a trend that's here to stay?
>
> Looking forward to hearing your thoughts!
>
> Best,
> [Name]

**Their reply** (50 minutes later): "sweet! How'd you come across it?" plus a real answer to the hiring question, and nothing on the other three.

*Why it worked:* the subject names the exact piece, so it reads as a reader, not a sequence → the second paragraph quotes their own wording back ("a 'when,' not an 'if'") and applies it to their own company, which proves the post was actually read → the questions are ones only they can answer → there's no ask, so replying costs them nothing and flatters their expertise.

*What to tighten next time:* four questions got one answer; ask one, two at most, and pick the one closest to the user's goal (here, the hiring question, which was also the one answered). Cut the first paragraph's "resonated" and the "not about X, but about Y" line; the second paragraph does that job with a real specific. Two em dashes become commas.

*The follow-through:* the user's reply agreed and added their own experience, but never answered "How'd you come across it?" When they ask a direct question, answer it first, in a line. It's an invitation to keep talking.

### Move: warm intro, credibility-forward, no hook

**Subject:** `Following up on [name 2]'s intro`

> Hi [Name],
>
> [Name 2] shared my resume with you a few weeks back about product opportunities at [Company]. I wanted to follow up with a quick intro.
>
> Most recently, I was [their most recent role, one sentence on the company and who it sold to].
>
> I built the product foundation from scratch: [three or four things, comma-separated].
> In [timeframe], before [the honest caveat — in the original, the company's funding trouble], I [one or two quantified outcomes].
>
> While [doing the thing that made them encounter the recipient's company], [Company] stood out as [the specific, sourced reason it stood out versus what they were building].
>
> My Background:
> - [credential]
> - [role at type of company]
> - [role at type of company]
> - [thing they built, described by what it does for whom]
>
> Between the domain and having owned product end to end, I think I'd be a strong fit at [Company].
> Would love to find 20 minutes of your time to chat.
>
> Best,
> [Name]

*Sentence by sentence:* no personal hook, and it doesn't need one — the warm intro is the permission structure, so the email spends its space on substance → the compliment is credible because it's *sourced* ("while researching the space at [previous company]") rather than asserted → volunteering the caveat reads as honest, not as an excuse → the bulleted background is scannable for a reader deciding whether to forward → the ask is bigger here, a real meeting, which the intro has earned.

### Move: long-time user of their product, proof, ideas on offer

*From outside the user's own emails: a freelance copywriter pitching a software company. Kept for its structure, not its voice. The same shape fits any pitch of services, a portfolio-backed job ask, or a partnership offer.*

**Subject:** `Hi [Name], I'm a [what you do, in their terms] looking to "wow" you`

> Hey [Name],
>
> I've been a user of your product for [how long, true and specific] and would love to have the chance to [the work you want to do for them]. I have:
>
> - [A quantified result for a named client: "Improved product sales by 15% for Company ABC"]
> - [A proven track record on the exact metric they care about, with proof links: "improving cart click-through (see screenshot [here] and [here])"]
> - [The bridge: used their tools for years on your own work, so you already have a few ideas for improving theirs]
>
> I'd like to discuss a few ideas I have for improving [the specific pages or surfaces]. I think you'll love them.
>
> When would be a good time for a short call?
>
> [Name]
> [Company]
> [Email]
> [Link]

*Sentence by sentence:* the opener is the connection, and it's one only a real customer can claim → the ask is stated in the first sentence, so the reader knows what this is → the bullets are scannable proof, each one checkable (a number, a named client, a link to evidence), which is the one place bullets beat prose in a cold email → the last bullet is the bridge and the give at once: their product made the sender good at this, so they arrive with ideas → one closing ask.

*What's constant with the other direct-ask emails:* a real, specific connection first; credibility kept short and concrete; something offered, not just requested; one ask.

*What to tighten, using what's in SKILL.md and `perspectives.md`:*
- **Subject.** It describes the sender, and it's long. It works better as a lowercase hook about them: `3 ideas for your pricing page`.
- **Show one idea instead of promising several.** Swap "I think you'll love them" for the single best idea, stated in a line: "your pricing page leads with features; your reviews all mention time saved." A concrete give earns the call; a promised one asks the reader to take it on faith.
- **The ask.** On a cold first email, asking whether they're interested beats asking for a time: "Want me to send the other two?"
- **"Would love to have the chance to"** can go. State the work plainly.
