# Job search

Interview follow-ups and thank-yous, cold and warm outreach to founders, hiring managers, and recruiters, check-ins when a process goes quiet, and rejection replies. Everything in SKILL.md applies; this file adds what's specific to getting hired.

Register: the user's own voice from `examples.md`. `hey [name],` for founders and cold outbound, `Hi [Name],` for scheduled-interview follow-ups and more formal recipients.

---

## Interview follow-ups (thank-you notes)

**What you need from the user:** what did they say that stuck? Not the topic, the specific claim, plan, or detail. "We talked about the roadmap" is unusable. "He said they want to own the operating system first, then build the agentic layer on top" is the whole email. Also worth asking: their role, screen or later round, whether anything came up that a project of the user's speaks to, and whether they named a next step.

Shape:

1. **Thanks.** One sentence, warm, no ceremony.
2. **The recall.** One to three sentences on a specific thing they said, and why it landed for the user. This is the email. Everything else is packaging.
3. **Optional artifact.** A link to something the user built, when it genuinely relates, introduced by *why it exists*, not the tech stack: "Here's something I built last month for a family member after hearing how he struggled to track expenses for his small business."
4. **Forward-looking close.** One line. "Looking forward to continuing the conversation!" / "Looking forward to the next steps!"

Four to eight sentences. A rich conversation earns three paragraphs; a twenty-minute screen earns four lines. Padding a thin conversation reads worse than being brief.

If they raised a concern about fit, answer it in one sentence, not defensively. Send the same day or the next morning.

**Several interviewers:** one note each, each with a different recall from *that* person's part of the conversation, under about 120 words. If two notes would be identical, ask the user what each person actually asked or said; recipients compare.

Angle splits for variants: lead with the strategic point they made / lead with the builder thread and attach a project / short and warm with no artifact.

## Cold and warm outreach for a role

### Who to email

| Company | Email |
|---|---|
| Under about 20 people | The CEO or a founder |
| Growth stage | The hiring manager or the VP over the function; a VP will route to a recruiter if needed |
| Large company | The hiring manager or a peer on the team (a practitioner whose work the user follows), not HR |

Hiring managers live in email; recruiters live on LinkedIn. After being rejected through the front door, a peer on the team is a legitimate side door. One person per company at a time; add the next only after a few days of silence.

A useful source of targets: the portfolio pages of investors who back companies the user admires.

**VC talent partners.** Connecting people to portfolio companies is their job. The email works like any other cold email: a reason to open it that's about them, short, easy to answer. What their job changes is which asks are on the table (see "Size the ask to their job" in SKILL.md). A broad ask like "any pointers on the best way to get connected with portfolio companies" is fine here, because pointing people somewhere is routine for them. Make the user easy to place: one sentence on what they want (stage, kind of role), three bullets a talent partner could paste into a note to a founder, links at the bottom.

*Example subject:* `been in your content orbit`. It's lowercase and sounds like a person. It tells her why a stranger is writing, and it makes her curious which of her content he means.

### Play signals specific to jobs

Direct ask when a relevant role is posted, they just raised and are hiring, there's a warm intro, or the user's background is an obvious match. Door-opener when nothing is posted, the recipient publishes ideas, or the match needs a conversation to become visible.

### Direct-ask moves

Angles that have worked, to spark ideas. They aren't a checklist; most emails use one or two, and the best one may not be on this list.

- **Interest, stated plainly, with the credential tucked in.** "Interested in being a part of what you're building (my background's as a PM but technical -> [credential])."
- **An ask that's easy to say yes to.** "Is there someone on the team who'd be better to connect with about PM roles?" beats "would love to chat": a busy person can forward it instead of scheduling. Asking whether they're *interested* outperforms asking for a specific meeting time on a first cold email.
- **Clear the obvious objection before it rules them out.** "Happy to relocate," "I know you're inundated right now," "I'll keep this to three lines."
- **Propose the role they haven't posted.** "Looks like you'll need a first sales hire soon" got founder interviews at companies with no opening. Only when the user can say why, from something real (a job post pattern, a stated plan, a stage).
- **Link work, don't attach a résumé** on the first cold email. A portfolio, a project, a writing sample.
- **Their words, turned into the ask.** Quote a line they've used about their product and give it a twist that points at the role. A founder once called his app "a friend in your pocket"; the opener became "now it seems like you're hiring PMs-in-training to be yours." That's the hook and the reason for writing in one sentence.
- **Interest with a date on it.** "Following you since Series B, when it was just a car insurance app" beats "big fan." Pair it with something the user built nearby at the time, so the interest reads as earned.
- **Their goal as the close.** Tie the ask to a number or mission they've stated ("one of the PMs who gets Jerry to 50m users. Worth a chat?"). Keep the line about what the user wants short and concrete. Abstract wants ("slope, ownership, learning") are about the user and could go in any email.
- **Optional closing question** that has nothing to do with hiring ("what's your all-time favorite coin?") when the hook was personal.

For warm-intro follow-ups, a short bulleted background block is fine: the reader is deciding whether to forward.

### Do the work first (the strongest differentiator)

The job-search cold emails with the most striking outcomes rarely asked for anything cold. They showed up with work in the company's own format:

- A résumé built as an issue of the company's newsletter (employee #13 at Morning Brew).
- A sample newsletter written unprompted, then a second one as the "follow-up" (hired).
- Months of community-building around a product before emailing its CEO a question and a doc of ideas (became Head of Community at Notion).
- A small fix to an open-source repo the company used (the CTO emailed *them*).
- A student interviewed a few of the product's power users, mapped where onboarding lost people, and sent three bullets plus one link to the deep dive. The team was working on onboarding that week, and the student had found problems they'd missed. No intern role was posted; she became their APM. (Why it worked, in the recipient's words, is in `perspectives.md`.)

When the user wants a company badly, ask: "Is there something small you could make in their format: a teardown, a mock of a feature, a fix, a sample of the work the role does?" Keep it proportionate (hours, not weeks), and remember spec work gets attention, not guaranteed offers. The email then introduces the work in a line and offers it without strings.

A follow-up that *is* a deliverable (the second sample, the teardown) beats any nudge. Keep it to three lines: what's attached, why ("figured showing you beats telling you"), and a question they can answer from a glance. "Is it close to what you're planning, or way off?" works because either answer is easy to give and both start a conversation.

### Open, missing, or changed: the role doesn't decide the email

Sometimes a role is posted, sometimes nothing is, and sometimes it was there and changed into something the user doesn't match on paper. The email works the same way in all three. Only the opening framing changes.

- **Posted and it fits:** say so in a line and spend the rest on the give and the proof.
- **Nothing posted:** treat that as room, not a dead end. Describe the work the user would do in concrete tasks, not a title. Only do this when the work they'd do comes from something real (what they saw in the product, a stated plan, the company's stage).
- **Posted, but the user is short on paper** (years, title, degree): name the gap yourself, in one plain sentence, before they do. Then give the one thing that matters more for *this* company, phrased as what it saves them ("wouldn't be learning your customers' world from scratch"). Owning the gap is what makes the counter believable. Hiding it makes them hunt for it.

Moves that hold across all three:

- **Proof beats claims.** "Another company wanted more experience than I had, and within four months I'd built their product function" answers the objection. "I'm a fast learner" doesn't.
- **Make yes cheap.** If the gap is real, offer a small, low-risk way to find out: a short paid trial, a project, a scoped first month. Spell out the easy exit. This only works with some existing relationship or strong proof. To a stranger with nothing else, it reads as desperate.
- **Leave a small idea with an out.** One or two lines on something for their customers, ending with "may or may not fit." It shows how the user thinks, and it doesn't make them respond to it.
- **Short beats complete.** Each paragraph does one job. If the reader can see the point in ten seconds, they'll assume the user works that way too.

## Recruiters

- Reaching out: recruiters triage fast. Role title or requisition, one-line fit with the strongest number, location/remote status, link. Under 100 words. LinkedIn is fine; they live there.
- After a recruiter screen: a short thank-you, one line confirming interest and anything they asked the user to send.
- Chasing next steps: see below. Recruiters are the right person to ask about timeline; hiring managers usually aren't.

## When the process goes quiet (ghosted after an interview)

- **They gave a timeline:** check in 1-2 business days after it passes.
- **No timeline:** about a week after the thank-you for early rounds, 7-10 business days after a final round.
- **Shape:** still interested (one line, with one specific from the conversation if the user has it), acknowledge that hiring takes time without groveling, ask for an updated timeline. Three to five sentences.
- **One more** a week later, by email. Then tell the user to move on mentally and keep the door open; "saw you launched X, congrats" months later is fine.
- **Channel:** stay on email, in the existing thread. A LinkedIn DM to an interviewer after a final round reads as impatient.
- **Competing offer with a deadline:** say so plainly. It's the one piece of news that legitimately speeds things up.

## Replying to a rejection

Within a day or two. Three to four sentences:

1. Thanks, with one specific (a person, a part of the process).
2. Accept the decision in one clause. No arguing, no asking them to reconsider.
3. The ask, plainly: keep them in mind for adjacent roles, or a line of feedback (easy to skip).
4. Short warm close.

No wounded tone, no "I was really hoping." Offer one variant that asks for feedback and one that doesn't.

## Never (job-specific)

- "I believe my skills and experience align well with the requirements"
- Restating the job description back at them
- "Any openings?" with no role or function named
- Explaining at length how you found their email address
