# Perspectives: reference ammunition

A collection of what practitioners, recruiters, investors, and datasets say about cold outreach. Some of it competes. That's deliberate: different readers and situations reward different moves, and knowing the range makes for better judgment than one rulebook.

**How to use this file.** SKILL.md and the scenario files are the defaults. Come here when a situation isn't settled by them, when the user asks "what works?" or "why?", or when choosing between angles for variants. When sources conflict, don't average them; pick the one whose conditions match this email and say which you leaned on in the note after the drafts.

Evidence tags: **[data]** a dataset or study (often sales email, correlation not causation); **[practitioner]** someone who receives or sends a lot of these; **[opinion]** a framework without published numbers.

---

## From the receiving end: a recruiter's take

A recruiter who gets dozens of executive cold messages a day described the message they never answer [practitioner]:

> "Hi Kristof, I hope this message finds you well. I am an executive in automotive with a successful career in growing sales. [10 more sentences explaining how good you are] I want to have a call with you to see how I can fit your clients. Please find attached my resume."

Their verdict: they stop reading at "I hope this finds you well" ("It found me bored"). The three fatal flaws:

1. **One-way street.** All take, no give.
2. **Zero targeting.** Wrong industry for the recipient.
3. **No differentiation.** Ten people send the same pitch every day.

"The sender is probably excellent at their job. But I'll never know. Because the message screams 'I need something from you.'"

What works instead:

- **Research first:** their industry, their clients, their content.
- **Make the ask tiny:** "15 minutes," not "a call to explore."
- **Show the win-win:** what does the recipient get from talking to the user? Their example of a give: *"I've seen a dramatic shift to robotic surgery in cardiovascular in the past 2 years. Happy to share what I learned."*
- Claimed response rates: generic outreach about 5%, targeted outreach 40-65%. "That's not a small difference. That's a different strategy."

The frame to carry: **not asking for charity, proposing a conversation between equals.** Messages that sound like business propositions, not sales letters. For the user, that means leading with something they know or have seen that's useful to the reader (an industry shift, a pattern across customers, a lesson from building in the space) before any ask.

## Send timing (sources disagree)

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| User-supplied guidance [data, source not named] | Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday are the best days; Monday best |
| Belkins [data, sales emails] | Tuesday-Thursday, 8am-12pm recipient time; small effect (0.54% vs 0.40%) |
| Hunter, 31M emails [data] | Send day showed no meaningful effect |

**Default for this skill:** early in the week (Monday through Wednesday, Monday first), morning in the recipient's time zone. Every source agrees the effect is small next to relevance and quality, so never hold a good, timely email (a post-raise note, an interview thank-you, an incident update) for a better day. Thank-yous go same day; incident updates go when promised; internal emails go when needed.

## Structure frameworks

Different shapes for different situations. All of them keep it short and about the reader.

- **Problem → cost → bridge → proof** (Hunter) [opinion]: name the reader's problem, show what it costs them, bridge to what the user offers in one sentence, add one sentence of proof. Good for partnership, sales-adjacent, and investor emails.
- **Three sentences** (Michael Seibel, YC) [practitioner]: the user's situation (assume half the reader's attention), why the reader should care, the ideal outcome as the ask.
- **Observation → insight → ask**: something you noticed about them, what it implies, the small ask.
- **Mouse trap** (Will Allred, Lavender) [opinion]: one trigger plus one value question, one or two sentences total. "Looks like you're hiring your first ops lead. Would it help to see how three similar teams set that role up?"
- **Four lines** (Becc Holland) [opinion]: premise (why them), hook, ask, and a push-pull line that gives them an out. Best premises, in order: content they created, content they engaged with, traits they claim in their profile. Cap the research at three premises in five minutes.
- **Trigger → problem it causes** (Jason Bay) [opinion]: "As you [trigger], [problem] usually shows up." Then a closed-ended interest question, under 80 words.
- **Poke the bear** (Josh Braun) [opinion]: a neutral question about something they may not have considered: "How are you handling X today?" Never a leading question.
- **Don't ask for the meeting; share information that makes them ask you** (Joseph Flaherty, Founder Collective) [practitioner].

## Personalization: three levels (Hunter) [opinion]

1. **Situation-specific**, no personal details: "Updating the marketing site is usually the last priority when you're focused on product." Scales to many similar readers.
2. **Trigger-event**: timely news or an industry shift: "With seed valuations tightening, most founders I talk to are trying to look more established than they are."
3. **Personal detail**, research-based: "Congrats on the Product Hunt launch last week, 580 upvotes is no joke." Best for high-value one-to-one emails, which is most of what this skill writes.

Related findings:

- Relevance beats personalization for senior readers. Directors and above tripled replies when the email referenced their company; personal details gave a smaller lift. Individual contributors responded more to personal details. Referencing something they did (a talk, a post) tripled replies. [data, Gong]
- 87% of buyers say cold emails don't address a challenge they actually have. [data, Gong]
- Mentioning one or two genuine similarities raises acceptance of requests from strangers. [data, Martin, Jacob & Guéguen 2013]
- "Relevance in the first line" rather than personalization for its own sake; 5 minutes of research, 5 insights, 5 minutes to write, one idea per line, under 125 words. [opinion, Kyle Coleman]
- Personal detail can live in a P.S., keeping the body clean. [data, secondhand, Lavender]

## Length (sources disagree)

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Hunter [data] | Two sweet spots: 61-80 words and 181-200 words |
| Boomerang, 40M emails [data] | 50-125 words got the most replies |
| Lavender [data] | Under about 100 words, readable on one phone screen |
| Steve Dalton, *The 2-Hour Job Search* [opinion] | Under 75 words for networking asks |
| Aakash Gupta [practitioner] | "If your cold email can't fit in a tweet, it's probably not good" |

Hunter's two peaks map onto SKILL.md's length table: the short peak is the hook-and-ask email, the longer peak is the warm intro or track-record email. Nobody's data supports the 300-word middle ground without a reason.

## The ask

- **Low friction, one ask.** Hunter's examples by use case: job seeking, "Would you be open to a quick coffee chat about the role?"; user research, "Would you have 20 minutes this week to walk me through how you handle this?"; recruiting, "Mind if I send over a candidate profile that might be a match?" [opinion]
- **Interest beats a time slot on the first cold email; a specific time wins once a conversation is going.** [data, Gong, 304K emails]
- **Offer something instead of asking for time**: in 85M emails, offer-style asks got more replies. For the user: a teardown, a data point, a short memo. [data, 30 Minutes to President's Club]
- **Ask for advice, not a job**, phrased as a question. Asking for advice also makes the asker look more competent. [opinion, Dalton; data, Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer 2015]
- **Name the size:** "15 minutes," not "a call to explore." [practitioner, recruiter above]
- **Give a reason.** Requests with a "because" get more compliance, at least for small asks. [data, Langer 1978]
- **An explicit out** ("totally understand if you're too busy") raised compliance in a meta-analysis of 42 studies, but a 2023 re-analysis of the least biased studies found no effect. Use it when it's natural, not as a trick. [data, conflicting]
- **No-oriented questions** ("Would it be crazy to...?") make saying yes feel safe. Chris Voss reserves the pushier "Have you given up on X?" for existing threads; as a cold opener it reads as manipulative. [opinion]
- People asking by email overestimate how many strangers will say yes; in person, askers underestimate. Plan for low odds per email, or find a warmer channel. [data, Bohns & Flynn]
- Asking for a meeting "today" instead of "next week" removed a 26% gap in professors' responses favoring white men. Near-term, specific asks can reduce bias in who gets answered. [data, Milkman, Akinola & Chugh]

## Words and tone

- **More than half the sentences about the reader.** "Every sentence should be about the recipient's situation." [opinion, Hunter; Dalton]
- **Simple reading level.** Third-to-fifth-grade writing got more replies than college level. [data, Lavender, Boomerang]
- **A little tentativeness helps** ("looks like," "might," "if that's useful"); gains fade past about five hedged sentences. This sits alongside `sounding-human.md`'s warning about stacked hedges: one light hedge reads human, a pile reads nervous. [data, Lavender]
- **Take a stance.** Slightly positive or slightly negative emails got more replies than neutral ones; opinionated beat bland. [data, Boomerang]
- **One question, not several.** Lavender found replies drop past one; Boomerang found one to three beat zero. One clear question satisfies both. [data, conflicting in detail]
- **Cut buzzwords** like "AI" and "platform" with executives, who reply less to cold email overall. [data, Gong]
- **AI-sounding email** bothers 69% of decision-makers; manually edited emails got 18% more replies than fully automated ones. Paul Graham has said AI-written founder emails feel like being lied to. [data, Hunter; practitioner]

## Follow-ups

- **"A follow-up is not a reminder."** Each email gives a different reason to engage and works on its own. [opinion, Hunter]
- **Shrink the ask each time:** a call, then "want me to send a 2-minute Loom instead?" [opinion, Hunter]
- **Three emails beat one:** 3.3% reply rate for a single email vs. 6.8% for a three-email sequence. [data, Hunter] (This skill's default of the first email plus two nudges is a three-email sequence.)
- **Space them 3-5 business days apart, in the same thread.** [opinion, Hunter]
- **Avoid** "Thoughts?", "never heard back," "following up," and bare "bumping this." [data, Gong]
- **3B7 routine** for job-search networking: no reply in 3 business days, contact the next person at that company; follow up with the first at day 7. People who reply fast are the ones worth prioritizing. [opinion, Dalton]
- **The breakup email** (a clear last note) gets the highest response in some sales sequences. [opinion, Becc Holland]
- **Persistence stories exist** (Tristan Walker's eighth email to Foursquare got the reply), but they're survivorship; each email in those stories added weight.

## Mechanics

- **Turn off open tracking:** 7.4% vs. 4.4% reply rate. [data, Hunter]
- **Send from a custom domain** where it fits (a company or personal domain): 5.2% vs. 2.5% from free email providers. For job seekers, a normal personal address is fine; this matters most for founders and anyone sending volume. [data, Hunter]
- **Small batches:** 21-50 recipients per sequence averaged 6.2% replies vs. 2.4% for 500+. One person per company at a time did better than several at once. [data, Hunter]
- **Benchmarks:** 4.5% average reply rate across 31M cold emails; headhunting 7.5%, sales 3%. A thoughtful one-to-one email should do far better; if it doesn't, the targeting is off. [data, Hunter]

## Give before you ask

- **Five-minute favors:** sharp product feedback, a useful intro, a relevant article. Generosity builds the relationship the ask later rides on. [opinion, Adam Grant, Adam Rifkin]
- **Close the loop after help:** thanks the same day, something useful a couple of weeks later with "no need to reply," then a note on what the user did with their advice. [opinion, Ramit Sethi]
- **Moderately weak ties** (people the user knows but rarely talks to) produced the most job moves in a 20M-user LinkedIn experiment, more than close friends or near-strangers. [data, Science 2022]
